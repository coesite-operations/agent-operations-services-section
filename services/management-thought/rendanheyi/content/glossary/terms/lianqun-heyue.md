A lianqun heyue (链群合约), or chain-group contract, is the single agreement a chain group holds to an end user. It is the instrument that makes a chain group one thing rather than a set of units that happen to be adjacent.

Its content is a commitment to a user outcome, a target for that outcome benchmarked against the best available anywhere, and a formula dividing the resulting value-added among the parties. Every micro-enterprise in the group signs the same contract. There is no separate agreement between the design unit and the manufacturing unit, no internal transfer price negotiated between them, and no internal customer whose sign-off releases payment.

The arrangement's force comes from what it removes. Under ordinary internal contracting, each unit's income is set by the unit downstream of it, which makes every handoff a negotiation and every negotiation a place where a unit can win while the user loses. The classic pathology is a supplying unit that meets its specification exactly, is paid in full, and has produced something the end user does not want — a perfectly discharged obligation and a failed product, with no party at fault. A single contract to the user makes this outcome impossible to construct: there is no intermediate specification to satisfy, because there is no intermediate payer.

The contract's second property is that it binds symmetrically. The value-added pool is shared when the group overshoots its target, and the shortfall is shared when it misses. A unit that performed its own part impeccably inside a group that failed the user is not paid for the impeccable part. This is the model's deliberate answer to local optimization, and it is also the arrangement's most demanding feature, since it requires units to accept exposure to outcomes they do not individually control. The model's position is that this exposure is the reason they coordinate.

The contract is entered voluntarily and can be declined. A unit that judges the target unreachable, or the split unfair, or the other members incapable, does not sign, and the group must find another unit or fail to form. There is no authority that can compel membership, which is what makes an unsigned contract informative rather than an act of insubordination.

The concept's boundaries: a chain-group contract is not a service-level agreement, which binds a supplier to an internal customer's specification. It is not a transfer-pricing arrangement, which is the thing it exists to abolish. It is not a partnership agreement among the units, since the units are not each other's counterparties — the user is. And it is not a performance target set by management, since no management party is present to set or to forgive it.
