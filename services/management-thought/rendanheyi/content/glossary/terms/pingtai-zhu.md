A pingtai zhu (平台主), platform owner, is the party responsible for a platform: a resource pool that micro-enterprises draw on rather than a unit that does work of its own. Design capability, manufacturing capacity, market access, and technical infrastructure have all been organized this way at Haier.

The position is defined by its grading rule, and the rule is the whole of the concept. A platform owner is compensated on the total value-added created by everything built atop the platform — including by parties outside the firm — and not on the platform's own revenue or margin. Nothing else about the position distinguishes it from ordinary internal ownership of a shared asset, and firms that adopt the vocabulary without the grading rule have adopted nothing.

The reason the rule carries the concept is that it settles the platform's characteristic conflict in advance. A party who owns a resource everyone must use, and who is graded on that resource's own margin, is being paid to charge for access, ration it, and prevent alternatives — which are the rational behaviors of a monopolist and the reliable outcome of internal platform ownership everywhere. The same party graded on what gets built atop the resource is being paid to lower the cost of access, remove the gates, and attract builders, including builders they do not control. The behavior follows from the measure rather than from the owner's disposition, which is why the model states the position as a compensation rule and not as a philosophy of stewardship.

The consequence is that the platform owner has no authority over what is built on the platform, and the model is explicit that they must not: a party who can veto what is built has the means to protect their position against the thing that would displace it. Haier's formulation is that the platform owner is not a boss but a resource provider, whose success is other people's success and who has no other kind available.

The position is where node micro-enterprises may arrive. Where a node's output becomes general enough that many units and outside firms build on it, the node's character changes, and the transition is marked by the grading rule becoming meaningful — there must be enough built atop it that total ecosystem value-added is a measurable quantity rather than an aspiration. Haier's central case is COSMOPlat, which began as internal manufacturing capability, served other Haier units as a node, and was subsequently exposed as an industrial platform serving unrelated industries.

The concept's history is a real source of confusion. Platforms and platform owners were the model's dominant organizing vocabulary in the period following the 2013–2014 restructuring, and the 2019 introduction of chain groups (链群) substantially displaced them without a clean public statement of what became of the earlier category. Accounts written before and after 2019 therefore describe the firm's structure in terms that do not reconcile, and neither is wrong about the period it describes.

The concept's boundaries: a platform owner is not a shared-services head, who is funded by allocation and graded on cost. It is not a platform product manager, who is graded on the platform's own adoption or revenue. It is not an internal-tooling lead, whose customers cannot leave. And it is not a general manager of the units on the platform, since holding authority over them is the specific thing the position excludes.
