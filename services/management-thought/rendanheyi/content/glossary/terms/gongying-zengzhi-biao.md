The gongying zengzhi biao (共赢增值表), rendered in English as the win-win value-added statement, is the accounting instrument a micro-enterprise keeps in place of a conventional profit-and-loss account.

Its premise is that the standard statement measures the wrong object for the model's purposes. A profit-and-loss account records transactions that have closed: revenue booked against cost incurred, over a period, for a legal entity. It is an excellent instrument for the question it answers and it is silent on the questions Rendanheyi requires answered — how many users the unit has as opposed to sales it made, whether those users are engaged with it or merely bought once, what other parties have joined the unit's ecosystem and what they contribute, and how much value now exists that the unit created but did not capture.

The statement therefore carries line items with no counterpart in conventional accounts. It records user resources — the count and character of the users attached to the unit, treated as an asset rather than as a source of past revenue. It records ecosystem parties: the outside firms, developers, and suppliers who have joined the unit's arrangement, and what each brings. It records value-added sharing, the division of surplus among all of these. And it records the divergence between value created and value captured, which the model treats as informative rather than as leakage.

The instrument's purpose is to make the difference between a transaction and a relationship visible in the accounts. Two units with identical revenue may differ entirely in whether their users will be there next year, and a P&L cannot distinguish them until the year after the difference has already cost the firm. A statement that carries users as a balance rather than as a flow makes that difference an accounting fact in the period it occurs.

The name's first two characters are load-bearing. 共赢 — win-win, mutual benefit — asserts that the statement accounts for value accruing to every party in the arrangement, including parties outside the firm, rather than for value extracted by the firm from them. A unit that improved its own margin by taking value from its ecosystem's other participants has, on this statement, gone backward, which is a result a conventional account is structurally incapable of reporting.

The instrument's honest weaknesses should be recorded. Its central quantities are not standardized, not audited, and not comparable across firms; user resources and ecosystem value are estimates whose method the reporting unit substantially controls. This makes the statement usable for internal steering and unusable as an external claim, and it is a fair criticism that a measure a unit defines for itself can be made to say what the unit needs. Haier maintains the conventional statutory accounts alongside it; the value-added statement supplements the P&L for the firm's own purposes rather than replacing it in the firm's reporting.

The concept's boundaries: the win-win value-added statement is not a balanced scorecard, which supplements financial measures with non-financial ones while leaving the financial measures as the object. It is not the value-added statement of European social accounting, which redistributes conventional P&L figures among stakeholder groups. It is not a set of KPIs or a dashboard, both of which are collections of measures rather than an account. It is an account, in the bookkeeping sense, of a different object than the firm.
