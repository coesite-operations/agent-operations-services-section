Daoshi (导师), "mentor," names an organizational role: an experienced member formally assigned to a specific newer member, responsible for that person's induction and growth over a sustained period.

The role is a formal assignment, not an affinity.
A named mentor is attached to a named mentee for a defined period, the relationship is recorded, and the mentor is answerable for the mentee's development.
This formality is the role's substance — an organization that hopes newcomers will find someone helpful has no mentor institution, because the newcomers who most need one are the least able to obtain one informally, and the result is that induction quality tracks a newcomer's luck and social confidence rather than the organization's intent.

Huawei's implementation, in place since the company's early years, assigns each new employee a mentor at onboarding who is responsible for their induction into the work and into the company's way of operating.
The company's distinctive move is what it does to the mentor.
The mentee's progress is counted in the mentor's own assessment, and a record of successfully bringing people on bears on the mentor's advancement — an employee who has not been a good mentor is understood to be short of a qualification for promotion.
This inverts the usual arrangement, in which mentoring is unpaid goodwill that competes with the mentor's real objectives and is therefore the first thing dropped under pressure.
When the mentee's growth is part of the mentor's record, the interests are aligned by structure rather than by exhortation.

The role's scope extends past task instruction.
The mentor transmits how the organization actually works — which is largely unwritten and cannot be conveyed by documentation, since the useful knowledge is exactly the knowledge nobody thought to write down.
This includes what the codified rules mean in practice, which conventions are real, and how work is genuinely conducted here as opposed to how it is described.
Huawei's formulation of this extends to the mentor's responsibility for the mentee's understanding of the company's values and direction, giving the role an ideological as well as a technical charge: the mentor is the channel through which the organization's self-understanding reaches a person who has just arrived and has not yet been shaped by it.

The mechanism is that the mentor is how an institution reproduces itself.
Codified process transmits what is written; internal instruction transmits what can be taught to a room.
Neither transmits the specific, situated judgment that a particular person needs at a particular point in their development, and no amount of documentation substitutes, because the newcomer does not yet know which question to ask.
A mentor is the mechanism for that residue, and organizations without one find their process compliance high and their practice hollow — everyone follows the rules and no one knows why.

The role's boundaries matter.
A mentor is not a manager: a manager directs the work and is accountable for its output, whereas a mentor is accountable for the person's growth, and the roles are held separately, since a person cannot safely bring their confusion to whoever rates their performance.
It is not an internal lecturer — lecturing is one-to-many, bounded, and organized around a subject; mentoring is one-to-one, sustained, and organized around a person.
It is not sponsorship or patronage, which advances a protégé's position; the mentor develops the mentee's capability and has no brief to advocate for them.
And it is not a coach engaged for a defined performance problem: the relationship is developmental, is assigned at entry rather than triggered by a deficiency, and is a standing feature of how people join the organization at all.
