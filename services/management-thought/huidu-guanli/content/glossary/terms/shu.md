Shu (術), "technique" or "method," names the level of concrete means: the specific tactics, administrative methods, and instruments by which an organization's institutions are operated and its ends actually accomplished.

In the classical Legalist division shu is the ruler's art of administration — the methods by which officials are appointed, tested against their stated duties, compared, and held to account.
Its classical hallmark is that, unlike law, it is not published.
Law must be public to work; technique often must not be, because a technique whose workings are fully known to those it is applied to can be gamed, and a method for detecting misreporting is worth little once its detection rule is circulated.
This asymmetry is the sharpest structural distinction between the two levels.

In management use shu is the whole repertoire of how: how a meeting is run, how a performance review is conducted, how a negotiation is opened, how a market is entered, how a metric is chosen. Huawei's concentration-of-force practice — massing overwhelming resource on a narrow decisive point rather than spreading it evenly — is shu, as is its personnel practice of promoting from those who have done the hard posting.

The level's essential property is that shu is instrumental and therefore disposable.
A technique is judged only by whether it accomplishes the end it serves, and when it stops working it is replaced without ceremony.
This is exactly what distinguishes it from institution, which is followed even when inconvenient because its value lies in being reliable, and from direction, which is not revised for performance at all.
The characteristic organizational failure at this level is promotion by success: a technique that works well is gradually treated as a rule, then as a value, until the organization is defending a method it can no longer say the purpose of.
The classical guard against this is the strict ordering — technique serves institution, institution serves direction — and the corresponding judgment that an organization long on shu and short on dao is clever and directionless, winning engagements it cannot say the point of.

The concept's boundaries matter.
Shu is not law: technique is held privately and revised at will, whereas law is published and binding until amended.
It is not positional advantage, which concerns the configuration one occupies rather than the moves one makes from it.
It is not direction, and cannot generate one — no accumulation of effective technique yields a purpose.
And in this frame the word carries no pejorative sense of manipulation; it names a legitimate and necessary level, and the objection in classical usage is never to having technique but to having only technique.
