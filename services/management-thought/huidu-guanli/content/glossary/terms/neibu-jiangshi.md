Neibu jiangshi (内部讲师), "internal lecturer," names an organizational role: a practicing member of the organization who teaches its people, as a duty attached to their expertise, rather than a professional trainer hired to teach.

The role's defining constraint is that the lecturer is currently doing the work they teach.
They are a working manager, engineer, or salesperson who instructs part-time and returns to their post.
This is the whole content of the role and everything else follows from it.
An internal lecturer teaches what they did last quarter, answers questions from cases they personally handled, and is accountable to an audience that can check their claims against the same reality — and that will encounter them again as a colleague.

Huawei is the developed case.
Its corporate university trains employees at every level, and its faculty is drawn substantially from serving managers and experts, with teaching treated as an obligation of senior position rather than an optional contribution — a manager's record as a lecturer bears on their standing.
Lecturers are certified through a defined qualification process, so the role is an actual institution with entry requirements rather than an informal request to present.
The governing pedagogical formulation is the integration of training and combat: instruction is built around real cases, frequently ones currently live, and is aimed at producing capability deployable immediately rather than understanding assessed by examination.
The related principle that generals are produced by fighting rather than by appointment expresses the same commitment — expertise is held to originate in doing, from which it follows that only those doing it can transmit it.

The mechanism is that this role is how an organization converts individual capability into institutional capability.
Experience acquired by one person is, by default, lost when that person moves on, and is inaccessible to everyone else in the meantime.
Requiring the experienced to teach forces that experience into transmissible form — and the forcing is the point, since articulating a practice well enough to teach it is what turns tacit competence into something the organization can hold, examine, and improve.
A second effect is that teaching audits the practice: a lecturer who cannot explain why a step exists has located a defect in the process, not merely a gap in their presentation.
The role therefore serves the same end as codified process, by a different route — process captures what can be written down, internal lecturing captures the judgment that cannot be.

The role's boundaries matter.
An internal lecturer is not a professional corporate trainer: a trainer's expertise is in instruction, and their material comes from elsewhere, whereas an internal lecturer's authority is entirely that they have done the thing, and would be destroyed by their leaving practice to teach full-time.
It is not a mentor, which is a sustained one-to-one relationship with a specific person's development; internal lecturing is one-to-many, bounded, and about a subject rather than about a person.
It is not a subject-matter expert consulted for answers — the duty is to transmit the capability, not to supply the output.
And it is not a reward or an honorific for seniority: it is work, with a qualification standard, that the organization requires of those who hold expertise it cannot afford to lose.
