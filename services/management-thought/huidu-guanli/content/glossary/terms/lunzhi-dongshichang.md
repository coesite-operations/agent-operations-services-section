Lunzhi dongshichang (轮值董事长), "rotating chairman," names an executive office that several peers hold in turn on fixed terms, each exercising the full authority of the organization's highest operating leader while their turn lasts.

The structure at Huawei, which developed it, works as follows.
A small number of senior executives — three, in the arrangement Huawei has run — take six-month turns as rotating chairman.
During a turn, the holder chairs the board and the executive management team and is the company's top operating leader.
When the turn ends, that person returns to being one peer among the others and serves under the next holder, whose decisions they are then bound by.
The practice began around 2004 with rotation inside the executive management team, was formalized in 2011 as a rotating chief executive office, and was restructured in 2018 into the rotating chairmanship.
It sits alongside, and is distinct from, a permanent chairman of the board who handles governance and legal representation but is not the top operating leader.

Several mechanisms are doing work at once.
Because a term is short and certain to end, no holder can accumulate a permanent faction or make the organization depend on their personal judgment; the office outlives every incumbent.
Because each holder must later serve under the peers who served under them, decisions get made with the knowledge that one will shortly be on the receiving end of the precedent one sets — reciprocity is structural rather than exhorted.
Because every rotation is a live rehearsal, succession is continuously tested rather than deferred to a crisis: the organization has already been run by each candidate, and knows how it went.
And because a holder cannot complete a long-horizon program within one term, programs must be institutional — carried by process and by the collective — rather than personal.

The office's boundaries matter.
Rotation is not division of labor: the holders do not split the company into portfolios and each run one.
Each holds the whole authority in sequence, which is precisely what makes the arrangement a check.
It is not ceremonial rotation of a title while a permanent leader decides; the holder genuinely commands during the turn.
It is not committee rule or consensus leadership — at any moment exactly one person holds the office and is answerable for it.
And it does not by itself resolve the founder question: rotation governs operational command, so it composes with, rather than replaces, a separate office holding authority over the organization's direction.
