Lang xing wenhua (狼性文化), "wolf-nature culture," names an organizational disposition modeled explicitly on the wolf, formulated at Huawei and widely propagated through Chinese business writing.

The canonical formulation gives three characteristics, which are always given together.
A keen sense of smell — acute sensitivity to opportunity, detecting it early and at a distance.
An indomitable, self-disregarding spirit of attack — once opportunity is scented, pressing without reservation and without being deterred by repulse.
And pack struggle — the wolf hunts in a group, and its capability is collective rather than individual; a lone wolf is not the image, and taking it for one inverts the concept.
The third characteristic is the load-bearing one and the one most often dropped.
The point of the metaphor is that an animal individually weaker than its prey succeeds by coordination, which is why the culture is offered as an account of how an organization without initial advantages defeats better-resourced incumbents.

In Huawei's practice the culture is associated with a set of concrete behaviors: putting sales and engineering staff physically into difficult and remote markets, promoting from those who took the hard postings, massing people onto a contested opportunity, and a general refusal to concede a market on the grounds that a competitor is established in it.

This term is genuinely contested, and both readings are recorded here rather than reconciled, because the sources do not reconcile them.

On the first reading, wolf culture names collective tenacity — a small organization's coordinated refusal to lose, which is admirable and is the reason Huawei exists at the scale it does.
On this reading the culture is about the pack, and the attack characteristic is directed outward at markets and technical problems.

On the second reading, wolf culture names an overwork regime: a euphemism under which exhaustion is recoded as spirit, an employee's inability to sustain the pace is recoded as a defect of will, and self-disregard — which the formulation states outright — is asked of people at a cost to their health and their lives.
Critics locate the culture in the extended-hours norms of the Chinese technology sector generally, and specifically in a series of Huawei employee deaths and suicides in the mid-2000s that prompted public controversy and internal reconsideration.
On this reading the attack characteristic turns inward, and the pack disciplines its own members.

The contest is not resolvable by choosing the more charitable reading, because the two are not describing different things.
They are describing the same practices from the position of the organization and from the position of the person inside it, and both descriptions are accurate.
Ren Zhengfei himself later distanced the company from the term, objecting that it had been reduced in popular use to mere aggression and stripped of the collective element, and Huawei's own later material de-emphasizes it.
That an organization's founder repudiated the most famous name for that organization's culture is itself part of the term's meaning.

The concept's boundaries matter.
Wolf culture is not individual ruthlessness or internal competition, which is the most common misreading and is close to the opposite of the pack characteristic.
It is not a synonym for hard work or high standards, which need no zoological metaphor.
It is not a formal Huawei doctrine of the same standing as its charter or its process institutions — it is a described disposition, popularized substantially by outside business writing, and subsequently disowned.
And it is not a term that can be adopted innocently: it arrives with the second reading attached, and an organization invoking it is invoking both.
